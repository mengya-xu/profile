FOOD ACCESS · PUBLISHED 2026

A 15-minute city—for whose household?

What can we learn when we start at the front door?

The promise of a 15-minute city is easy to picture: everyday needs within a short trip from home. But a neighborhood average can hide very different experiences from one household to the next.

The work

In this study, we used GIS to examine food access for more than 3.7 million households in Los Angeles County, considering door-to-door travel, terrain, and travel time. The aim was to identify households facing poor access within socioeconomically disadvantaged communities.

Why it matters

Household-level measurement helps make the uneven geography of everyday access more visible.

GIS · Household-level accessibility · Los Angeles County

Xu, M., de la Haye, K., MacDonald, B., & Wilson, J.P. (2026). Rethinking food access at the household level under the 15-minute city framework. Cities 170,106657.

MOBILITY · PUBLISHED 2026

Food access on the clock.

How does food shopping fit into an actual day?

Getting groceries is a trip we fit around the rest of life. Where we can go depends on the time available, the ways we travel, and the needs of the household.

The work

This work extends the space–time prism for multimodal, household-level accessibility modeling, with a GIS framework applied to food access in Los Angeles.

Why it matters

Bringing time and transportation into the picture offers a way to ask more realistic questions about access.

GIS · Space–time prism · Multimodal accessibility

Xu, M., Livings, M.S., de la Haye, K., & Wilson, J.P. (2026). Extending the space–time prism for multimodal, household-level accessibility modeling: A GIS-based framework applied to food access in Los Angeles, CA. Applied Geography.192:104054.

EVERYDAY MOBILITY · PUBLISHED 2024

Where do we actually buy our groceries?

Does the nearest-store map capture everyday shopping?

People’s routines do not stop at neighborhood boundaries. I wanted to understand grocery shopping through the places people actually visited.

The work

Using aggregated mobile-phone mobility data from 2021, our team examined grocery store visits among residents east and northeast of Downtown Los Angeles. Only 13% of visits were within residents’ home census tracts.

Why it matters

The findings point to the limits of defining food access only within a residential boundary, and show how mobility data can add a view of everyday behavior.

Aggregated mobility data · Spatial analysis · Grocery shopping

Xu, M., Wilson, J.P., Bruine de Bruin, W., Lerner, L., Horn, L., A., Livings, M.S., & de la Haye, K (2024). New insights into grocery store visits among East Los Angeles residents. Health & Place 87, 103220.

HEALTHY AGING · ONGOING RESEARCH

The places where we grow older.

How do unequal environments shape health as we age?

The neighborhoods where we grow older bring together social conditions, access to resources, and environmental exposures. My current work asks how these conditions relate to differences in health.

The work

Through postdoctoral research at USC, I have worked with Medicare claims, cohort, mobility, and geospatial data to study neighborhood environments, dementia, chronic disease, and health disparities. Related manuscripts on spatial social polarization are under review in the August 2026 CV.

Why it matters

The goal is to move toward questions about modifiable environments and opportunities to reduce disparities. These projects are ongoing; no final findings are presented here.

A cooler, greener walk.

What does the urban environment feel like at walking scale?

My earlier research explored park accessibility, walkability, and social inequalities. A newer project follows this interest into the thermal environments people encounter on foot.

The work

A manuscript in preparation explores pedestrian-scale thermal mapping through image relighting, using a GeoAI approach to study greenspace cooling in Los Angeles and London.

Why it matters

This is a developing research direction. The illustration is conceptual, and does not represent measured temperatures or study findings.

COLLABORATIVE RESEARCH · FOOD SYSTEMS

Making food-system data useful.

How can research help people make decisions?

A research project can also become a resource for the people working on the issues it studies. That connection has been part of my work on food access in Los Angeles.

The work

As a member of the USC team, I contributed to Food Base LA, a public-facing interactive dashboard hosted by the LA County Office of Food Systems. I also co-authored policy reports on healthy food access in Eastside neighborhoods and food insecurity during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Why it matters

These collaborations connect spatial research with food-system planning, policy, and community decision-making.

Collaborative research · Public-facing data · Policy reports

USC research team, in collaboration with the LA County Office of Food Systems. Policy reports: Improving Healthy Food Access in Four Eastside Los Angeles Neighborhoods (2023); Enough to Eat (2021).
